Teaching reflection · August 2026

What changes when a PBL course moves beyond its fixed track?

This year marks the third iteration of our PBL collaboration with a university partner in Washington. During the first two years, the course followed a relatively stable path: lectures established a common frame, and project work developed within a set of known technical and educational boundaries. That structure was useful. It gave international teams a shared starting point and made a complex program teachable within limited time.

In the third year, we introduced something new. Alongside the established lecture track, teams were given room to pursue more open-ended development—to extend the given work, make more of their own decisions, and attempt outcomes that could not be specified completely in advance.

The difficulty was not simply that the projects became harder. The nature of the learning task changed.

From completing a path to constructing one

In a fixed track, many important decisions have already been made by the teaching team: what counts as progress, which dependencies come first, how far a prototype should go, and where students should ask for help. Open development transfers part of that responsibility to each group. Students must not only solve a problem; they must frame it, reduce it to a workable scale, coordinate uneven knowledge, and decide what to abandon.

The problems that emerged across groups should therefore not be read only as implementation failures. They exposed the hidden work of project-based learning: scoping, expectation alignment, task ownership, integration, risk management, and communication across different academic and cultural habits. More freedom made this work visible.

Freedom is not the absence of structure

My current reflection is that an advanced PBL component needs a different kind of structure from a lecture. It should not prescribe the answer, but it should make the decision process observable. A team may benefit from declaring a minimum viable outcome and a separate stretch goal; identifying its largest uncertainty early; keeping a short decision log; and meeting checkpoints that examine assumptions rather than only finished functions.

This also changes the instructor's role. Intervention should not mean taking the project back from the students. It can mean asking whether the group has reduced the problem enough, whether responsibilities are actually understood, whether a technical obstacle is masking a coordination problem, and whether the current plan still fits the time that remains.

A possible shape for the next iteration

For a future course, I would like to distinguish two connected layers more explicitly. The core layer would preserve the common lectures and guided development that every team needs. The extension layer would operate more like a small studio: teams propose a direction, name the uncertainty they are taking on, and receive feedback at short design reviews. Progress would be documented not only by what was built, but by how the team revised its question and its plan.

This is not yet a conclusion or a universal model. It is a working note from a course that changed its own conditions in its third year. The value of this year's difficulties may be that they show us more clearly what must be taught when students are asked to move from following a project to authoring one.

Research outcome · April 2026

Different media produce different modes of engagement

A common assumption in immersive experience research is that a larger or more enveloping display should produce “more engagement.” Our museum study suggested a less linear interpretation.

Original artworks, immersive projection, and interpretive video did not simply differ in emotional intensity. They supported different styles of engagement.

The display-based video produced higher arousal and fast-band activity. The 180° projection supported a calmer, presence-oriented absorption. Direct viewing of the original artwork reflected a more internally regulated form of engagement. For exhibition design, this suggests that digital and original formats should not be ranked on a single scale. They may be complementary tools for different modes of attention and interpretation.

Organizing · 26 May 2025

Hosting a Belgian academic delegation at SIT

A delegation visit can look effortless in the final group photograph. The work behind it is spatial, editorial, technical, and interpersonal.

For the visit of 31 researchers and faculty members from the Walloon academic community—joined by representatives connected to the Embassy of Belgium in Tokyo—I prepared a guidebook, mapped posters and demonstrations across two research spaces, coordinated presentation themes, and helped translate the laboratory’s work into an accessible Society 5.0 narrative.

The program connected mental-health research, physiological emotion estimation, VR-based spatial evaluation, care robotics, smart environments, and industry–academia projects. The purpose was not only to display outputs, but to make future collaboration imaginable.

Fieldwork · 1–4 April 2025

What a laboratory cannot reproduce about a museum

During visits to RMFAB, Musée L, UCLouvain, and the Louvain Aging Brain Lab, the research question expanded from “can EEG measure emotional response?” to “what must a measurement method tolerate in order to remain meaningful in a cultural environment?”

At the Bruegel Box and during Slow Art, signal quality depended on hair and sensor contact; posture and facial movement introduced artifacts; light, noise, open space, and social distraction changed attention; and unfamiliar equipment affected participants during setup. These were technical problems, but they were also evidence about the conditions under which cultural experience occurs.

The fieldwork reinforced a methodological position I continue to develop: interpretability in real settings can matter more than a clean classification result obtained under artificial conditions.

Invited talk · 29 May 2025

Shared artistic experiences at Osaka Expo 2025

At the Belgium–Japan Workshop during the Wallonia Science and Innovation Days, Sébastien Lugan and I presented “Cognitive and Emotional Stimulation for Aging Brains through Shared Artistic Experiences at the Royal Museums in Brussels.”

The talk connected physiological sensing with a larger question: how might museums and shared art experiences support older adults and people with cognitive or expressive challenges? The aim was not to claim that art can be reduced to a biomarker, but to explore how non-verbal evidence may complement observation, conversation, and inclusive experience design.
