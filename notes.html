Research outcome · April 2026

Different media produce different modes of engagement

A common assumption in immersive experience research is that a larger or more enveloping display should produce “more engagement.” Our museum study suggested a less linear interpretation.

Original artworks, immersive projection, and interpretive video did not simply differ in emotional intensity. They supported different styles of engagement.

The display-based video produced higher arousal and fast-band activity. The 180° projection supported a calmer, presence-oriented absorption. Direct viewing of the original artwork reflected a more internally regulated form of engagement. For exhibition design, this suggests that digital and original formats should not be ranked on a single scale. They may be complementary tools for different modes of attention and interpretation.

Organizing · 26 May 2025

Hosting a Belgian academic delegation at SIT

A delegation visit can look effortless in the final group photograph. The work behind it is spatial, editorial, technical, and interpersonal.

For the visit of 31 researchers and faculty members from the Walloon academic community—joined by representatives connected to the Embassy of Belgium in Tokyo—I prepared a guidebook, mapped posters and demonstrations across two research spaces, coordinated presentation themes, and helped translate the laboratory’s work into an accessible Society 5.0 narrative.

The program connected mental-health research, physiological emotion estimation, VR-based spatial evaluation, care robotics, smart environments, and industry–academia projects. The purpose was not only to display outputs, but to make future collaboration imaginable.

Fieldwork · 1–4 April 2025

What a laboratory cannot reproduce about a museum

During visits to RMFAB, Musée L, UCLouvain, and the Louvain Aging Brain Lab, the research question expanded from “can EEG measure emotional response?” to “what must a measurement method tolerate in order to remain meaningful in a cultural environment?”

At the Bruegel Box and during Slow Art, signal quality depended on hair and sensor contact; posture and facial movement introduced artifacts; light, noise, open space, and social distraction changed attention; and unfamiliar equipment affected participants during setup. These were technical problems, but they were also evidence about the conditions under which cultural experience occurs.

The fieldwork reinforced a methodological position I continue to develop: interpretability in real settings can matter more than a clean classification result obtained under artificial conditions.

Invited talk · 29 May 2025

Shared artistic experiences at Osaka Expo 2025

At the Belgium–Japan Workshop during the Wallonia Science and Innovation Days, Sébastien Lugan and I presented “Cognitive and Emotional Stimulation for Aging Brains through Shared Artistic Experiences at the Royal Museums in Brussels.”

The talk connected physiological sensing with a larger question: how might museums and shared art experiences support older adults and people with cognitive or expressive challenges? The aim was not to claim that art can be reduced to a biomarker, but to explore how non-verbal evidence may complement observation, conversation, and inclusive experience design.
